# Older-Adult Individual Interview Guide

**Session:** 45–60 minutes | **Session type:** `older_adult_individual` | **Research ID:** `WA-###`

**Purpose:** Learn about ordinary meal decisions and alternatives from the older adult's own perspective. This is problem discovery, not a service conversation. Seek accounts that support, complicate, and disconfirm the assumptions in `01-assumption-evidence-map.md`.

## Facilitator setup (before the participant joins)

- Confirm the participant has received the information sheet in `04-consent-and-privacy.md`, has a `WA-###` ID, and that contact details are stored separately.
- Check the recruitment tracker for useful broad contrasts (for example, a satisfied routine, cooking frequency, or current alternatives). Do not mention tracker answers as facts or assume a problem.
- Prepare a non-recording note-taking option. Do not record names, precise places, diagnoses, detailed health histories, or unrelated family information in analytical notes.
- If an urgent safety concern or unnecessary sensitive disclosure arises, pause rather than probe and follow the approved process in `04-consent-and-privacy.md`.

## 1. Consent and opening (5–6 minutes)

Read the researcher introduction and complete the granular consent script in `04-consent-and-privacy.md`. Confirm that participation, audio recording, diary participation, anonymised quotations, and future contact are separate choices. Record each applicable choice using `yes`, `no`, `withdrawn`, or `not_asked`.

Say: “We are interested in everyday meal decisions and family support, including routines that are working well. You can skip any question, take a break, or stop at any time. This is research, not a service.”

**Recording check:** Ask the separate audio-recording question before starting any recording. If the answer is no, continue with notes only. Ask about video only if it has actually been proposed; otherwise record `not_asked` in the session consent record.

Start with rapport that does not frame age, ability, or decline as the topic:

1. “What have you been enjoying or spending time on lately?”
2. “What is a typical day like for you these days?”
3. “What kinds of food or meals do you generally enjoy, if any come to mind?”

**Do not lead:** Do not ask the participant to diagnose themselves, explain why they cook more or less than someone else, or defend their routine.

## 2. Recent meal reconstruction: last seven days (8–10 minutes)

“Thinking back over the last seven days, please walk me through your meals in whatever order is easiest. Include meals you made, bought, received, skipped, repeated, or did not have to think much about.”

For each day or memorable meal, lightly map:

- What was eaten or chosen, when the decision happened, and who was involved, if anyone.
- What happened immediately before the choice; what made this option the one used.
- Whether it was planned, repeated, changed, or a default.
- What effort, time, shopping, travel, preparation, clean-up, cost, or coordination was involved, if relevant.
- How the participant felt about the result: satisfied, neutral, disappointed, or something else.

**Probes:** “What happened next?” “How often does a day like that happen?” “Was that typical or unusual?” “What other option did you consider, if any?”

**Do not lead:** Do not label a repeated, ready-made, delivered, restaurant, family-supported, community, or simple meal as a failure. Do not imply that cooking is the preferred outcome.

## 3. Two Recent decisions in depth (10–12 minutes)

Ask for two specific decisions from the reconstruction. Let the participant choose; if helpful, ask for one that went as intended and one that did not.

### A. A decision that went as intended

“Please take me back to that specific meal decision. What happened from the first moment you thought about it through to the end?”

Probe for trigger, steps, choices considered, people involved, effort and trade-offs, result, and whether the participant would choose the same approach again.

### B. A decision that did not go as intended

First ask: “Thinking about the last seven days, was there a meal decision that did not turn out as you wanted?” If a recent decision exists, select it for this reconstruction. Do not replace a known decision that did not go as intended with a satisfactory example.

Probe for the concrete sequence, consequence, frequency, workaround, and what changed afterward. Ask: “How important was that to you?” and “Did you want anything to be different?”

If the participant reports no recent decision that did not go as intended, record that absence explicitly as disconfirming/no-problem evidence. Then, separately ask: “Tell me about a recent meal decision that was simply fine or satisfactory. What made it work?”

**Disconfirming prompt:** “Are there times when meals are working well enough that there is no problem to solve? What makes those times work?”

## 4. Current routine and alternatives (8–10 minutes)

“Tell me about the ways you currently handle meals when you do not make something from scratch.”

Explore, only where relevant:

- Cooking for one or for others; effort, portion sizes, leftovers, repetition, planning, and clean-up.
- Grocery acquisition: shopping, ordering, carrying, timing, convenience, and any routine that works.
- Ready-made or delivered food, restaurants/takeout, family assistance, community meals, convenience options, and mixed routines.
- What is dependable, enjoyable, too much effort, too costly, inconvenient, or entirely satisfactory.

Ask: “Which of these options works well enough for you now?” “What trade-offs, if any, do you accept?” “If you changed nothing about your meals, what would that be like?”

**Do not lead:** Do not contrast the participant's routine with an imagined ideal or presume that portions, repetition, grocery routines, or cooking alone are burdens.

## 5. Previous attempts and family context (6–8 minutes)

“Have you ever tried to change any part of your meal routine, or decided not to? Please tell me about one specific time.”

Explore what prompted it, what was tried, effort or cost, outcome, why it continued or stopped, and whether the result mattered to the participant.

Then ask: “When family has been involved with meals or practical help, what has felt useful, neutral, annoying, or controlling—if any of those fit?” Follow with: “What made it feel that way?” and “What choice did you feel you had?”

Include the valid no-involvement case: “It is also fine if family is not involved. What is that arrangement like for you?”

## 6. Privacy and sharing boundaries (4–5 minutes)

Use relationship situations, not a proposed solution: “When you make everyday meal choices, what—if anything—would you be comfortable telling family or another supporter?”

Ask separately:

- “What would you prefer to keep to yourself?”
- “Are there things you would share only if you chose to in that moment?”
- “What would make sharing feel helpful, intrusive, or like pressure?”
- “If someone wanted more information than you wanted to share, what would you want to happen?”

Check understanding in the participant's own words: “How would you describe the boundary that matters most to you?”

**Do not lead:** Do not suggest that family access, proof, updates, or oversight is normal or necessary.

## 7. Disconfirmation, importance, and Close (4–5 minutes)

Ask all of the following even if earlier answers suggested difficulty:

1. “Are you satisfied with cooking less, cooking rarely, or not cooking at all? What makes that work for you?”
2. “Is there anything about your meal routine that you do not see as a problem, even if someone else might?”
3. “What would be evidence that no change is needed?”
4. “If there is something you would like to change, how important is it to you right now: not important, a little important, somewhat important, very important, or extremely important? What makes you say that?”
5. “How ready, if at all, do you feel to try a change in your own routine? What would make you less or more ready?”

Close: “What have I missed about your ordinary meal decisions or the role other people play?” Thank the participant. Reconfirm any diary or future-contact choice without treating either as required. Remind them they may later withdraw using the approved contact process.

## Facilitator evidence notes

During the session, separate a verbatim/reported account from your interpretation. Record recency, concrete trigger, actions, current alternative, outcome, participant ownership, and a plausible alternative explanation. Do not treat an adult child's view as evidence in this session.

| Assumption IDs | Listen for | Evidence type(s) most likely | Disconfirming evidence to preserve |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `P1`, `P2`, `W1` | Participant-owned desired outcome; repeated meal decision, trigger, frequency, consequence | `reported_behaviour`, `attitude` | Satisfied routine, rare issue, no desired change |
| `P3` | Non-clinical routine, planning, access, cooking-for-one, shopping, or coordination factors | `reported_behaviour` | Need is mainly outside this study's non-clinical scope; log only minimum necessary boundary note |
| `U1` | Routine and contrast context relevant to a possible segment | `reported_behaviour`, `attitude` | No meaningful difference from adjacent routine/context |
| `B1` | Current alternatives, costs/trade-offs, what already works | `reported_behaviour` | Alternative fully resolves the participant-owned need |
| `R1`, `R2` | Invited/helpful support, rejected help, choice, mismatch in roles | `reported_behaviour`, `attitude` | Pressure, control, conflict, or no shared problem |
| `T1` | Participant's own explanation of wanted and unwanted sharing | `attitude`, `reported_behaviour` | Cannot state a boundary, feels unable to refuse, or rejects sharing |
| `C2` | Past time, money, effort, or trade-off; continuation condition | `reported_behaviour` | No prior sacrifice and no reason to continue a change |

## Post-session Debrief and evidence capture (same day, 8–10 minutes)

1. Check consent states and recording status; store notes under `WA-###` only. Keep direct identifiers out of the debrief and matrix.
2. Write separate entries for: (a) exact or near-verbatim reported behaviour, (b) your observation, and (c) researcher interpretation. Use only `observed_behaviour`, `reported_behaviour`, `attitude`, `third_party_inference`, or `researcher_interpretation` for `evidence_type`.
3. For each atomic item, record `session_type=older_adult_individual`, assumption ID(s), recency, `source_strength`, `parent_owned` (`yes`, `no`, or `unclear`), `confidence`, current alternative, alternative explanation, and contradictory evidence. Use `none identified in this item` only when true for that one item.
4. Capture at least one item, when present, for each of: a recent behaviour, a working alternative, a family-boundary account, and a disconfirming or satisfied case. Do not manufacture a negative case when none was discussed.
5. Note a follow-up or diary invitation only if separately consented. Do not infer interest in a paired session from this interview.

**Self-check:** Did I introduce a solution, frame lower cooking as a deficit, mistake satisfaction for denial, or record interpretation as fact? If yes, correct the notes and flag the limitation.
